Guild Was 'Comparable' to Gibson and Fender, but Cost Less: Kim Thayil on Why He Chose a Used S-100

UG Exclusive: Although "there were a few people who specifically use Guilds," Thayil went with this choice and is still sticking with the brand and model to this day.

Back in the day, for Soundgarden's Kim Thayil, Guild's double-cut SG-styled S-100 was a better choice than the usual Gibson and Fender models. As he told Ultimate Guitar in a recent interview, Guild "wasn't in the business of doing endorsement deals," and as it wasn't widely used, making it a reliable and more affordable option.

In 2026, there's a plethora of cost-friendly and reliable guitar models, although you still have to do some searching. But back in the 1970s and the 1980s, it wasn't usually easy to dig something up, and plenty of players went with the usual Fenders and Gibsons. Young Thayil found a used S-100 for $250 in 1978 while browsing for a suitable axe. Still owning this old black double-cut to this day, a few years ago, Thayil teamed up with Guild for his signature model.

In promotion of his book "A Screaming Life: Into the Superunknown with Soundgarden and Beyond," we sat down with Thayil, where he explained how this decision to buy the S-100 came to be.

Kim Thayil on Choosing Guild S-100 over Gibson and Fender

UG: You've talked about not being able to afford a Fender or Gibson at the time, and a salesman pointing you toward a used Guild as the affordable option. At what point did that practical compromise turn into a deliberate identity? Where you're not just playing a Guild because it's what you could afford, but because it's specifically what you want?

Kim Thayil: "Well, it was used, so I imagine I could have found an affordable used Gibson or Fender at that time. What they told me was that the Guild was comparable to Gibsons or Fenders. They weren't in the business of doing endorsement deals, whereas Fender and Gibson had powerful brand recognition because of the endorsement deals they had developed with so many popular artists."

"And Guild just kind of didn't do that. I think there were a few people who specifically use Guilds. I can't remember at the time, but a few jazz guys and a few pop guys. So, because of that, I could get a guitar that was of comparable merit for less price. And it was used, but it was in really great shape, so that worked."

"I think utilizing it over a period of time for songwriting, and then playing it in a few bands with it: Identity Crisis, and a lot of social situations, jamming with other people in their garage or in their backyard or whatever, you gain a familiarity with it. As I'm getting older, maturing, coming into my own from your teen years, where you're trying your best to emulate things you hear on record or radio, and think that the standard of being good is be able to play 'Rock and Roll All Nite' by Kiss, or play 'Stranglehold' by Ted Nugent. That was a big accomplishment. But then, try to play it well. What does that mean? So, you go through all those things."

"Then you start writing your own songs, you start playing with your friends, and everyone has their own particular personality, and the way they sing or play guitar, drums, and you kind of try to accommodate them, and they're accommodating you, so that together you can execute this song that you want to play. Whether it's one that you wrote or whether it's a cover that you're doing. Y'know, if you're doing some Ramones song or some Kinks song or a Beatles song. And people show you things and you learn it."

"So, you play it on your instrument, and your instrument begins to become more and more familiar – just like your mitt does, if you ever played Little League or baseball, and your mitt becomes sort of part of your uniform. It's not just your clothing, it's not just your apparel, it's also like your armor, y'know? And the mitt's interesting, and guitar is interesting, is that it's armor, it's apparel, it's a tool, it's a paintbrush, and it can be the palette and the canvas, as well."

"I don't know when that occurred, but probably when someone tried to get me to use some other guitars, I was like, 'Eh,' and you go back to the one that feels comfortable, and that's what it was. So, probably sometime before Soundgarden formed, maybe in the early 80s, I started developing a familiarity and affinity for its behaviors. And really, within the first few years of Soundgarden, I was doing things that were particular to the way the guitar was constructed, and the way it would voice some of those…what would be considered sonic aberrations, I've given voice to that with this guitar. It's like, 'Oh, we can do this with this lineup, this band. These can be components of our playing and of our songwriting.' That's when I married myself to that instrument."

The "Jesus Chris Pose" Guitar Trick

UG: You've described picking behind the bridge, sliding the fretting hand around the 12th fret to get that pulsating harmonic effect. Is there a particular Soundgarden track where that approach is doing the heaviest lifting? Where you'd point someone to hear exactly what the S-100 makes possible that another guitar wouldn't?

Kim Thayil: "'Jesus Chris Pose', I think. I'm playing beneath the bridge, and at the same time, sliding my fingers up and down around the 12th fret. So, some combination of this squealy picking from behind the bridge mixed with harmonics. It's a cool effect. But there are other things – I can bend strings and then pick behind the bridge, and it just sounds like an insane bat that's coming at you. Or maybe a bottle rocket that had a will."
